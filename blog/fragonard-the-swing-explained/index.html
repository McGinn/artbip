Fragonard's The Swing Explained: The Bishop Who Never Appeared

The story of an indecent request that one painter refused and Fragonard accepted, and how to read the picture he made: the slipper, the hat, the statue and the man in the shadows.

Jean-Honoré Fragonard, The Swing, c. 1767–1768. Wallace Collection, London.

In October 1767 the playwright Charles Collé got back to Paris and heard a piece of gossip worth writing down. The painter Gabriel-François Doyen told him that a "gentleman of the Court" had sent for him. Doyen found the man at his petite maison, the discreet house kept for pleasure, with his mistress beside him. The gentleman pointed at her and explained what he wanted: a picture of Madame on "a swing that a bishop would push", with himself placed where he could see her legs.

Doyen was the wrong man to ask. He had just made his name at that year's Salon with a huge altarpiece of Saint Geneviève saving Paris from a plague, painted for the church of Saint-Roch. He told Collé the proposal left him "confounded and petrified". Once he had recovered, he sent the gentleman to a colleague. Collé's entry ends with the news that Fragonard had taken the job and was already at work on "this singular painting".

That journal entry, now quoted on the Wallace Collection's in-depth site about the picture, is why The Swing has a story at all. It tells us who was asked first, roughly when the painting was begun, and what the client had in mind. What it leaves out is his name.

A bishop was requested

The patron has long been assumed to be François-David Bollioud de Saint-Julien, an immensely rich tax farmer for the French clergy. A man who collected money for the Church might well have wanted a bishop in his joke. But Collé never names him, and the Wallace Collection is careful to say that a name "continues to remain elusive".

Whoever he was, he got most of what he asked for. Look at the lower left. A young man lies back in the bushes, one arm flung up, gazing straight up at the woman on the swing. The art historian Thomas Crow reads him as the patron himself, placed exactly where he asked to be.

The bishop did not make it. Look into the shadows on the right and you find instead an older man holding the ropes, smiling, half lost in the dark. The Wallace Collection describes him as her elderly husband. One swap turns a private joke about the clergy into a love triangle: a husband pushing, a wife flying, a lover hiding below.

The slipper is the punchline

Now follow the pink dress up to the top of its arc. One little shoe has come off and is sailing away to the left, a pale fleck against the trees. The museum's description of the picture is plain about the result: the force of the swing throws off her slipper and gives the lover below a privileged view. His delight, it says, is suggested by the hat he holds out towards her.

That view meant more then than it might now. An essay on the clothes in the picture, by the dress historian Jacqui Ansell on the Wallace Collection's site, explains that a woman of the 1760s wore a linen shift next to her skin and nothing underneath it. Bare legs in slippers would normally be seen only by her intimates. The lover's angle is the whole point.

Ansell spots a quieter joke too. The young man waves a stiff tricorn hat. Near the older man sits a soft, shapeless one. She asks, as a question rather than a claim, whether we are meant to read that contrast as a comment on the "cuckolded husband".

Cupid is keeping the secret

On the far left, on a stone pedestal, a winged child raises a finger to his lips. This is Étienne-Maurice Falconet's statue Menacing Cupid, made for Madame de Pompadour, Louis XV's mistress, and shown to great acclaim at the Salon of 1757. The original is now in the Louvre. A Cupid calling for silence is a fitting guard for a secret meeting, and anyone in 1767 who knew whose statue it was would have caught the second reference.

The Pompadour echo runs through the colour as well. Fragonard had been the star pupil of François Boucher, and the Wallace Collection points out how closely The Swing recalls Boucher's 1759 portrait of Pompadour, which hangs in the same museum: blue-green foliage setting off a beribboned pink dress. The swirling diagonals and the hard contrast of pink against blue-green are Boucher's vocabulary, pushed into something wilder. Fragonard sets it all in an overgrown garden, with more statues and a distant fountain half hidden in the trees.

Swings were a cure as well as a game

A swing was not an innocent prop in 1767. An essay on the museum's site by the historian Javier Moscoso traces swings back through the rituals of ancient Greece, Chinese festivals and the margins of medieval manuscripts. In Europe they were long thought of as something for the sick. Swinging was first recommended for fevers, gout and kidney pains. By the 18th century it was being tried against melancholia, seasickness and sexual impotence, and swings were often found in brothels.

At the same time they were high fashion. Swings appeared on wallpaper, fans, playing cards and tea services. Moscoso calls Fragonard's painting perhaps the best-known example of a craze that spread around the world.

A painter who had just walked out

The commission also caught Fragonard at a turning point. In 1765 he had been the success of the Salon with Corésus and Callirhoé, a vast scene of sacrifice from Greek myth that the Crown bought to be made into a tapestry. He seemed set for a career painting history.

Then at the Salon of 1767 he showed The Swarm of Cupids, a ceiling study with no story at all. Denis Diderot called it an "omelette", and other critics were just as savage. Fragonard never exhibited at the Salon again. He stepped away from the Academy and began painting very different pictures for patrons the Wallace Collection describes as wealthy, worldly and more interested in pleasure than duty. The Wallace Collection suggests that Doyen's choice of Fragonard shows his unhappiness with the Academy was already common knowledge. The Swing is one of the first fruits of that break.

The Louvre said no

The picture was made to be seen by a few people in an intimate room. A century later it was famous. When the duc de Morny's collection was sold in Paris in 1865, the catalogue described the scene as unfolding in a park "enriched with statues", and the 4th Marquess of Hertford bought it. By then it was already one of the best-known French paintings in existence.

Six years earlier, in 1859, the Louvre had been offered it and turned it down. The museum's pages don't record why. Ansell can only ask whether ideas of dress and decorum had something to do with it. By the late 1850s women wore drawers under their long skirts, she notes; a hundred years earlier they had not.

What the cleaning found

Between August and November 2021 the painting was cleaned for the first time in over a hundred years. It rarely left public display, and the varnish had turned badly yellow while old retouching showed through. It was the first painting the Wallace Collection had ever conserved on site, under the conservator Martin Wyld.

Removing the yellowed varnish and old overpaint made the whites and pinks of the dress crisper, deepened the flush on the young man's face and brought back the shimmering blue of the older man's coat. The surface had suffered remarkably few losses.

The study also caught Fragonard at work. There was very little underdrawing, which suggests he painted straight onto the canvas with confidence. And there were small changes, noted for the first time: the woman's knee and petticoat were adjusted, and so was the young admirer's right shoulder. Look at that shoulder, and at the knee lifted into the light, and you are looking at places where Fragonard changed his mind mid-stroke.

That is the pleasure of a picture like this. In a gallery you get the pink dress and the flying shoe in a few seconds and move on. Live with it a little longer and the rest arrives: the finger on Cupid's lips, the soft hat, the husband's smile in the dark, the twin statues of children below the swing, the leg Fragonard reworked.
