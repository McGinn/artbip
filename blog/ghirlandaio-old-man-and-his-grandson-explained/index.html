An Old Man and his Grandson Explained: Drawn from a Dead Man

Why Ghirlandaio's tender double portrait began with a drawing of a corpse, what is wrong with the old man's nose, and why nobody knows who the two of them were.

Domenico Ghirlandaio, An Old Man and his Grandson, c. 1490. Musée du Louvre, Paris.

The Nationalmuseum in Stockholm owns a drawing of an old man with his eyes shut. It is done in silverpoint on paper prepared with a pink ground. The head sits bolt upright. The lips are pressed together and the thin hair is a mess. Nothing about the face moves.

This is the man you see in the Louvre, bending warmly over a small boy. Ghirlandaio drew him first, and according to the Metropolitan Museum's 2011 catalogue The Renaissance Portrait from Donatello to Bellini, he drew him dead.

The painting looks like a picture of affection. It may also be a memorial.

Asleep, or dead?

The first scholar to write about the Stockholm drawing was Bernard Berenson, in 1903. He thought it looked as if it had been made just after the old man died, while he still lay in his bedclothes. Thirty years later he changed his mind and said the man was only napping.

The Met catalogue sides with the first opinion. The frozen face, it says, is "certainly that of a corpse". The textbook by Hartt and Wilkins, History of Italian Renaissance Art, describes the sheet simply as a drawing of the old man on his deathbed and concludes that the painting served as a commemoration.

What Ghirlandaio did next is the clever part. A dead face cannot look at anyone. So for the painting he tilted the head, and the old man now seems to be communicating with the child, as a living person would. A change of angle is enough to bring him back.

The nose

You see the nose before anything else. It is swollen, knobbly and ridged, and Ghirlandaio has not softened a single bump. The condition is rhinophyma, a disease of the tissue of the nose that mainly affects elderly men.

Berenson, never short of a phrase, said it looked "like a potato that has started budding". The Oxford Dictionary of Art and Artists praises the picture's "ruthless realism". Not everyone liked it. The scholar Adolfo Venturi found it too realistic for Ghirlandaio, whose manner he thought of as sweet, and gave it instead to the painter's brother Davide.

Yet the realism is not cruel. Hartt and Wilkins argue that the deformity is studied with such respect that it stops looking ugly, and the boy proves the point. He looks straight past the nose and up into the old man's face, one small hand laid against the red, fur-trimmed robe. If the child does not mind, why should we?

Look at how the two are set against each other. The old man is shown in three-quarter view, with his heavy face and grey hair. The boy is in profile, with long fair curls and a red cap, his features idealised. The Met catalogue calls the picture an important step in the Florentine portrait, beyond the old habit of painting sitters in strict profile. It shows faces, and it also hints at what the two people are like.

Who were they?

Nobody knows. The usual English title calls the boy a grandson. The Louvre is more cautious: its title, Portrait d'un vieillard et d'un jeune garçon, means simply portrait of an old man and a young boy. The Met catalogue reads the bond as plainly familial, an elderly father or perhaps a grandfather looking at the future of his family line. Hartt and Wilkins say the boy is possibly a grandson, and that the sitters have never been identified.

Plenty of names have been tried. A 19th-century history said the painting came from the Frescobaldi family; a 20th-century one said the Ridolfi. The Met calls both reports unsubstantiated. Soon after the Louvre bought it, the old man was taken for Francesco Sassetti, a general manager of the Medici bank whom Ghirlandaio painted several times. That idea was refuted in 1907, and the Met adds that Sassetti's skull was a different shape, yet it keeps resurfacing. Nor is the child the Sassetti boy in a similar double portrait now in New York: that boy looks about ten or eleven, and this one no more than about four.

The Met's own suggestion, made for the first time in 2011, is Giovanni Tornabuoni (1428–1497), brother-in-law of Lorenzo de' Medici and a patron Ghirlandaio worked for again and again. Ghirlandaio painted him twice in the murals of Santa Maria Novella, finished in December 1490, at the age of sixty-two. He also painted him in a room of the Villa Lemmi, seated at a table with his nine-year-old daughter Ludovica standing at his side. It is an attractive idea. It is still only an idea.

A picture for the family

Whoever they were, the painting was not made for the public. The catalogue points out that portraits in church murals could be seen by anyone in Florence, but Ghirlandaio's double portraits were private, painted for family and friends. It brings in Leon Battista Alberti, who said that portraits make the absent present to their friends, even long after the sitter has died.

The background deserves a look too. Through the window at the upper right a road winds towards a building with a slender tower at the foot of a wooded hill, and beyond it a tall, pale blue rock. The critic Federico Zeri thought the boy and the landscape were routine enough to be an assistant's work. The Met disagrees, and quotes the critic Roger Fry, who in 1909 called the picture "Ghirlandaio's supreme masterpiece".

Scratches, Berlin and the Louvre

The painting surfaced in the 19th century with the Florentine dealer Stefano Bardini. In 1879 he wrote to the director of the paintings gallery in Berlin that it was "the only portrait known in Florence of that era that is available for sale". The two sides apparently never agreed on a price, in what the Met catalogue calls a transaction full of intrigue. In 1880 the Louvre bought it from Bardini instead.

Wilhelm von Bode, writing about Berlin's museums, later gave a reason why they did not buy it despite lengthy negotiations: the picture was damaged. Hook-shaped scratches ran across the old man's forehead and his left eye. An Italian edition of Cavalcaselle and Crowe's history of Italian painting had already reported in 1896 that a harsh cleaning had stripped away some of the paint, without mentioning the scratches. They stayed visible for most of the 20th century, until a restoration in the early 1990s painted them in.

Today the panel, poplar wood and 62.7 by 46.3 cm, hangs in the Grande Galerie of the Louvre's Denon wing.

Living with it

In a busy gallery it is easy to give this picture a few seconds, enough to register the nose and move on. It rewards slower looking. Live with it for a while and the eye starts to drift: from the nose to the boy's hand, from the hand to the warm red of the robe, out through the window to that small blue rock.
