American Gothic Explained: A Sister, a Dentist and a Gothic Window

Who the two people in Grant Wood's painting really were, where the house stands, and how a picture meant kindly came to be read as a joke.

Grant Wood, American Gothic, 1930. Art Institute of Chicago.

The woman in American Gothic was not a farmer's wife. She was Grant Wood's sister, Nan, and in real life she looked nothing like the woman in the picture. The Art Institute of Chicago's curator of American art, Sarah Kelly Oehler, describes Nan as young, pretty and stylish, with fashionably marcelled hair. The man with the pitchfork was Wood's dentist, Dr B. H. McKeeby, who was older and rather closer to the part. The two never stood side by side. Each posed for Wood separately; they were never in his studio together.

So the most famous couple in American art is not a couple, and possibly not even a pair. Who they are meant to be, and what Wood thought of them, is the real story of the painting.

A window seen from a car

It started with a house. In the summer of 1930 Wood was in Eldon, a small Iowa town, for a month-long community art project run by Edward Rowan, director of the Little Gallery in Cedar Rapids. One afternoon John Sharp, an art student from Eldon who knew Wood through the gallery, took him for a drive. Wood spotted a small white wooden house with a pointed, church-like window in its gable and asked Sharp to pull over so he could sketch it.

That house is still there. According to the American Gothic House Center in Eldon, Wood later said he found the window amusing and called it "pretentious" for such a small house. The Art Institute's curator tells it slightly differently: Wood wondered who would live in such an outdated house, and decided it would be "American Gothic people". He had his title.

The style is called Carpenter Gothic, and it was popular in the 1880s: quickly built wooden homes given steep gables, vertical board-and-batten siding and pointed windows borrowed from churches. The window in Eldon had a practical side Wood probably never knew about. It is hinged, the house centre explains, so beds and dressers could be passed into the upstairs rooms, because the staircase inside was too tight. The building is listed on the National Register of Historic Places as the Dibble House, after Charles and Catherine Dibble, its early owners, and it now belongs to the State of Iowa.

Building the picture in the studio

Wood sketched on the spot but painted in his studio in Cedar Rapids, and the finished picture is far more arranged than it first looks. Some of his preliminary sketches show the house on its own, which is why the Eldon centre believes the house came first. When the figures arrived, they changed as he worked. In one early sketch the man holds a rake. Wood gave him a three-tined hayfork instead.

The red barn behind the man's shoulder was added. So were the two potted plants on the porch behind the woman, a snake plant and a beefsteak begonia, which were not there when Wood drew the house. Both plants appear in his 1929 portrait of his mother, Woman with Plants, and so do two things the woman in American Gothic wears: an apron edged with rickrack and a cameo brooch at the throat.

Once you know that fork was a choice, you start to see it everywhere. Look at the man's bib overalls: the stitching repeats the three prongs and carries the shape on into the pattern of his shirt. Turn the fork upside down in your mind and it matches the panes of the window above. The window itself is two arches joined at the top, and the two heads below it, side by side under one roofline, repeat that shape.

Oehler points out that Wood tied each figure to a part of the farm. The woman belongs to the house: even her hair echoes the plants on the porch, and the little circles on her apron reappear in the curtains of the upstairs window. The man belongs to the barn and the work of the fields.

Wood had learned this crisp, patterned way of painting only recently. In 1928 he went to Munich to supervise the making of stained-glass windows he had designed, and saw early Netherlandish paintings there. The Oxford Dictionary of Art and Artists says the encounter moved him away from his earlier Impressionist style; he has been called "the Memling of the Midwest".

Father and daughter, probably

The question everyone asks is whether they are married. The Art Institute's own description of the painting calls them a farmer and his daughter, dressed and posed, in Wood's phrase, like "tintypes from my old family album." The Eldon centre notes that in one rare explanation Wood himself said they were father and daughter.

But Wood rarely explained his work, and he did nothing to settle the matter. Oehler says the relationship is genuinely unclear, that the age gap upset early viewers, and that Wood left the ambiguity alone partly because the argument was good publicity. Look at their eyes: he stares straight out at you, gripping the fork, while she looks away to the side. The picture gives you no more than that.

A prize, and a storm

Wood sent the finished painting to Chicago, to the Art Institute's annual exhibition of American painting and sculpture, which opened on 30 October 1930. It won the Norman Wait Harris Bronze award and $300. Oehler notes it was described as third place but was nearer fifth if you rank the cash prizes. The Art Institute's Friends of American Art bought it and gave it to the museum that same year.

Then the arguments started. Some East Coast critics read it as a portrait of rural backwardness. Housewives in Iowa started a letter-writing campaign to say they looked nothing like this and were not backward: they had tractors and modern farm equipment. The Oxford Dictionary records that at first the picture was condemned by some as an insulting caricature of plain country people.

Wood said he meant something else. The Art Institute writes that he intended a positive image of rural American values, offering reassurance at the start of the Great Depression. The Eldon centre quotes him calling it an "affectionate portrait". Oehler's own verdict sits between the camps: there is satire in it, she says, but "it's very loving; it's very gentle."

The little human touches

Once the first stare wears off, the painting softens. A single loose curl hangs behind the woman's right ear, the one break in her severe hairstyle. The man fastens his collar with a gold button, a slightly showy touch on so sober a figure. And at the bottom right, on the overalls, Wood signed and dated the picture in light blue paint: GRANT WOOD 1930.

The stern faces may have a simple source. The Eldon centre suggests Wood borrowed them from early photographs, when long exposures meant photographers discouraged sitters from smiling, and from the travelling photographers who used to pose families in front of their homes. Oehler has her own theory about why the picture has been parodied endlessly: the faces are so blank that anyone can be dropped into them.

Living with it

That blankness is also why American Gothic is worth living with rather than glancing at. In a gallery most people check the faces, smile at the fork and move on. On a screen you see every day, other things surface in their own time: the curl, the collar button, the plants that weren't really on the porch, the signature hiding in the stitching.
